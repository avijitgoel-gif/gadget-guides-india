## Read speed is on the box. Write speed is what you wait for

Every listing above leads with read speed. Copying files onto the drive, which is what you sit and wait for, uses write speed, and that is usually much lower.

SanDisk's own datasheet for the Ultra USB 3.0 says "Up to 130MB/s read speeds. Write speeds lower." US retailer B&H lists the 32GB version of the same SDCZ48 drive at up to 40MB/s write. An independent review of the 128GB SDCZ48 by Gough's Tech Zone measured writes of roughly 24 to 40MB/s depending on the test, against reads above 150MB/s.

So treat the Amazon.in spec field that shows "Write Speed: 100 Megabytes Per Second" on the SanDisk Ultra 64GB with caution. It does not match SanDisk's datasheet.

For a rough sense of the gap, here is simple arithmetic for copying a 4GB folder of photos (4,000MB):

| Write speed | Time for 4,000MB |
| --- | --- |
| 4MB/s (HP v236w listing) | about 17 minutes |
| 30MB/s (typical budget USB 3.0 write, per the review above) | about 2 minutes 13 seconds |

Real times depend on file size, the port and the PC. Thousands of small files copy slower than one big file.

## Buying guide: three things that confuse pen drive buyers

### Why does my 64GB pen drive show only about 58GB?

Drive makers count 1GB as 1,000,000,000 bytes. SanDisk's datasheet footnote says so and adds "Actual user storage less", and HP's brochure says some capacity is used for formatting. Windows counts in units of 1,073,741,824 bytes, so 64 billion bytes shows up as about 59.6GB before formatting overhead. A reading in the high 50s is normal, not a fake drive.

### Why won't a 5GB file copy to my pen drive?

Most pen drives ship formatted as FAT32. Microsoft's documentation gives FAT32's maximum file size as 4GB minus 1 byte, and a Microsoft engineer's blog explains that Windows reports this as "The parameter is incorrect" rather than a clear message. Back up the drive and reformat it as exFAT, which Microsoft's file system specification lists with a maximum file size of 16 exabytes. Most TVs and car stereos read exFAT, but check yours first, as some older ones only read FAT32.

### Is USB 3.0 worth it if my PC has blue and black ports?

USB-IF, the body behind the USB standard, defines USB 2.0 at 480Mbps and USB 3.2 at 5Gbps and above. A USB 3.0 drive in a USB 2.0 port works but drops to USB 2.0 speed, as HP's brochure notes. On most PCs the USB 3.0 ports have a blue insert or an "SS" logo. Use those.

## Be careful with very cheap high capacity drives

The same bestseller page showed little known 128GB and 256GB "metal" drives for ₹550 to ₹999 with 1 or 2 ratings each. ### Is 64GB enough for a pen drive?
For documents, music and photo transfers, yes. At these prices 64GB USB 3.0 drives cost ₹899 to ₹999, so 32GB only makes sense if you spend under ₹700 and move small files.

### Why is my pen drive so slow when copying?
Write speed is far lower than the read speed printed on the pack. SanDisk's datasheet for the Ultra USB 3.0 says write speeds are lower than its 130MB/s read, and a USB 2.0 drive like the HP v236w lists only 4MB/s write. Plugging a USB 3.0 drive into a USB 2.0 port also slows it down.

### Can I copy a file larger than 4GB to a pen drive?
Not if the drive is formatted as FAT32, which has a 4GB minus 1 byte file size limit according to Microsoft. Reformat it as exFAT after backing up the data.

### Which pen drive works with both phone and laptop?
A dual connector drive such as the KEXIN Dual 64GB at ₹902, which has USB-A and USB-C ends. It works with USB-C phones, including USB-C iPhones and iPads, but has a one year warranty.
