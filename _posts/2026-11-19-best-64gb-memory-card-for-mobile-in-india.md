## What the labels on a memory card mean

### Class 10, U1 and V10: the same 10MB/s

The SD Association, which sets the card standards, defines each speed class by its minimum sustained write speed. Kingston's guide to the classes puts it plainly: C10 and U1 both mean at least 10MB/s, and if a card shows one logo and not the other, you still get 10MB/s. V10 is also 10MB/s.

### U3 and V30: at least 30MB/s

U3 and V30 both mean at least 30MB/s sustained write. Kingston says a 30MB/s minimum write spec is "perfect" for a smartphone card, and U3 is the usual requirement for 4K video recording. The Biwin MS100 and CP PLUS 64GB both carry U3 and V30. The SanDisk Ultra, KEXIN U1 and Amazon Basics cards are 10MB/s class cards.

### A1: the rating that matters for apps

A1 is the SD Association's Application Performance Class 1. SanDisk's datasheet gives the A1 figures as 1,500 random read IOPS and 500 random write IOPS. In plain terms, A1 cards handle the many small reads and writes that apps and photo galleries make. If you will move apps to the card, pick one with A1 printed on it.

### The big read number

The headline "140MB/s" is a maximum read speed. SanDisk's datasheet says it needs a compatible reader and that write speeds are lower.

## Watch out for ₹999 "128GB" cards

On the same bestseller pages, several unbranded "128GB" cards were listed at ₹998 to ₹999 with ratings of 1.0 to 1.9 stars from a handful of buyers. SanDisk's own 128GB Ultra GO was ₹2,099, and its 64GB Ultra was ₹1,600. When genuine 64GB cards from known brands cost more than an unknown 128GB card, be suspicious.

Fake cards are a known problem: they report a bigger capacity than they have, and files saved past the real limit get corrupted. The free open source tool f3 (Fight Flash Fraud) fills a card with test data and checks it reads back correctly, which shows whether a card lives up to its claimed capacity. Run it, or a similar full write test, on any card that seems too cheap, while you are still inside the return window.

## Buying guide: four checks before you buy

### Check that your phone takes microSDXC

The SD Association defines cards over 32GB and up to 2TB as SDXC, which use the exFAT file system. Cards up to 32GB are SDHC with FAT32. Almost all recent phones with a card slot accept SDXC, but an old phone, dash cam or camera that only supports SDHC will not read a 64GB card. Check the maximum card size in your device's manual.

### Do not plan on "internal storage" on a Samsung

Android's adoptable storage lets some phones format a card as internal storage. Samsung's support page says adoptable storage is not available on Samsung devices. You can still save photos and media to the card.

### Expect about 59.6GB, not 64GB

SanDisk defines 1GB as 1,000,000,000 bytes. A 64GB card therefore holds about 64 billion bytes, which a phone or PC counting in binary units shows as roughly 59.6GB, before formatting overhead. This is normal and not a sign of a fake card.
